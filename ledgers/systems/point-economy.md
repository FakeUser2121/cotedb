### y1v1 p.30–32 — Initial point grant & core economic rules
[rule] (p.30–31) Complete institutional isolation: ANHS is an enclosed boarding campus. Students are prohibited from leaving the grounds or contacting outside individuals (including family and friends) without administrative permission.
[rule] (p.31–32) Point currency parity: 1 Private Point = 1 Japanese Yen (1 pt = ¥1). All on-campus transactions (cafeteria, convenience stores, mall, equipment, services) are conducted exclusively with points via digital mobile terminals.
[rule] (p.32) Monthly point grant: homeroom teacher Chabashira announces that 100,000 points are deposited into each student's account on the first of every month, ostensibly reflecting their merit and evaluation for passing the entrance exam.
[rule] (p.32) Transferability & anti-extortion: points may be freely transferred between students via terminals. Coercion, extortion, and theft of points are strictly prohibited and monitored by school authorities.
[rule] (p.32) Non-convertible & graduation expiration: points cannot be converted into real-world cash, and all unspent points revert to the school upon graduation.
[rule] (p.52) Dormitory utility policy: dormitories (single rooms, keycard access) do not charge or deduct points for water, gas, or electricity consumption.
[trivia] (p.32) Deliberate administrative trap: the school deliberately conceals the connection between student conduct, class evaluation (Class Points), and monthly deposits, allowing Class D to foolishly assume 100,000 points is guaranteed free money.

### y1v1 p.106–109 — Free survival supplies & safety valve mechanism
[rule] (p.106–109) The zero-point safety valve: the school store features a dedicated lower-shelf section of **entirely free goods**:
- Free basic sanitary items, toilet paper, notebooks, pencils, and emergency food rations.
- Allocation rule: students are permitted to take up to one package per month free of charge.
- Institutional deduction: the school designed this infrastructure from inception anticipating that certain students would exhaust or lose all their private points, ensuring basic survival without cash or points.

### y1v1 p.120–130, 138–139 — The S-System unmasked: Class Points, zero-point baseline, & Class A monopoly
[rule] (p.120–122) The S-System evaluation deduction: the monthly deposit is directly tied to the collective evaluation of the class. On May 1st, Class D receives **0 Private Points** due to April disciplinary infractions:
- **98 absences and late arrivals**
- **391 incidences of talking or mobile phone usage in class**
- Total penalty completely wipes out the baseline 100,000 point monthly allowance.
[rule] (p.125) Initial grade-wide Class Point standings (May 1st, 2015):
- **Class A:** 940 points (94,000 Private Points/student)
- **Class B:** 650 points (65,000 Private Points/student)
- **Class C:** 490 points (49,000 Private Points/student)
- **Class D:** 0 points (0 Private Points/student)
[rule] (p.124–125) Proprietary evaluation secrecy: the school refuses to disclose the exact formula or weighted rubric used for point deductions and rewards, comparing it to corporate employee evaluations.
[rule] (p.125) The zero-point trap (the ratchet baseline): stopping negative behavior (tardiness, talking) merely halts further deductions; it does **not** grant positive points. Once a class hits 0 points, it remains at 0 points the next month unless affirmative merit points are actively earned.
[rule] (p.126) Meritocratic class sorting: students are sorted upon entrance into four tiers based on overall competence. Class A represents the most capable elite; Class D is the bottom depository for failures (chabashira: "You're defective. This is just the result of you being defective.").
[rule] (p.127) Dynamic class promotion: class letters are not fixed. If Class D accumulates more points than Class C, Class D immediately becomes Class C and vice versa.
[rule] (p.127) Zero-point survival guarantee: students with 0 points do not starve; housing in dormitories and basic food rations remain guaranteed by the school.
[rule] (p.128–129) Red-mark instant expulsion: receiving a single failing score (red mark) on any midterm or final examination results in **immediate, irreversible expulsion** from the school.
[rule] (p.129) Class A monopoly on privilege: the school's renowned guarantee of **100% employment and college matriculation** applies **strictly and exclusively to graduates of Class A**. Classes B, C, and D receive no placement assistance from the school and are left entirely to their own devices.
[rule] (p.138–139) Terminal point transfer mechanism: private points can be transferred bilaterally between students directly through their school-issued smartphones by entering the recipient's phone number.

### y1v1 p.152, 173–174, 178, 197–199 — Midterm pass threshold & expulsion-avoidance economics
[rule] (p.173) Midterm pass threshold: the exact pass line is **32 points** — scoring at least 32 is safe, anything below is a red mark (→ immediate expulsion). Sudou asks for the exact cutoff; Ike confirms it to him.
[rule] (p.152) High marks feed class assessment: per Hirata's announcement, strong midterm scores should improve the class's overall evaluation, on top of preventing expulsion.
[rule] (p.173–174) Study-group scoring policy: Horikita sets the target at **50 points for everyone**, refusing to coach them to merely skate by at the 32-point threshold ("Aiming to just skate by is dangerous").
[rule] (p.178, 197–199) The dead-weight calculus: expelled failures raise the class average test scores without further effort; Horikita argues that dumping the failures leaves only the better students, making a higher class rank simpler to attain — the position she defends to Ayanokōji in their night debate and concedes is "basically correct" only after he proves her blind to others' unique strengths.

### y1v1 p.229–233 — The old-test gray market
[rule] (p.230–232) Past exam papers are openly tradeable: no school rule bars studying from old test problems; point transfers are logged but legal, and barter among students (even across years) is routine enough that a third-year negotiates without surprise.
[trade] (p.230–231) Ayanokōji buys a third-year Class D student's first-year midterm answer sheet (plus the post-admission mock answers as a bonus): opening offer 10,000 → seller asks 30,000 → AK shows 20,000 → settles at **15,000 points**, transferred in advance (transfer logs serve as mutual anti-fraud insurance).
[rule] (p.230) Zero-point students are the natural sellers: excluded from cafés, they eat the **free vegetable meal set**, making cash points the ideal lever (AK targets his stakeout specifically by watching who buys the free set).
[rule] (p.232–233) Old-test value model: identical mock papers across years prove the test bank repeats verbatim — old papers are "insurance," not a guarantee for the midterm.
[trade] (p.234–235) Distribution strategy: withhold the papers from the trio until the day before the test, then release online to trigger an overnight class-wide cram; Kushida fronts the story (papers "from a third-year student you're close to") so Ayanokōji stays invisible.

### y1v1 p.253–254 — Buying an exam point (the 100,000-point sale)
[rule] (p.253) Test points are purchasable in principle: on admission students were told they can buy anything with points, and "the midterm test is just one more 'thing' at this school" — Chabashira neither denies the mechanism nor cites any rule against it.
[trade] (p.253–254) Price discovery for a single English test point: **100,000 points** ("the exceptional price" for a first-ever request). Ayanokōji and Horikita each contribute via student-ID transfer — combined 100,000 points — and Sudou's failing mark is re-scored to a pass on the spot (expulsion cancelled).
[fact] (p.253) Practical scarcity: no student retains a spare 100,000 points weeks into the term — only a two-payer coalition can afford the transaction.
[note] (p.253–254) The sale is half-comic but fully binding: Chabashira takes both IDs, collects, and treats the result as official ("There's nothing more to be done.").

### y1v1 p.257–258 — No points for the worst class
[fact] (p.257–258) Class D carries the **lowest average score of all four classes by far** after the midterm → Horikita's verdict: no point allocation next month either.
[fact] (p.258) Ground-level poverty: after spending their points, Ike and Yamauchi live off the school's free offerings — the zero-balance survival mode the warning track promised.
[rule] (p.258) Default expectation set in-voice: don't count on monthly points in Class D; "learn to live a more modest life and give up on points" (Horikita).
[note] (p.258) The class's first explicit long-game framing: point acquisition + exam performance are separate axes — "Studying isn't the sole factor in deciding who goes into what class."
