Chapter 1: Chabashira Sae’s Soliloquy p.10–12

Setting. No scene — pure first-person interior monologue, volume cold open. Chabashira narrates; the only other presence is an unnamed "certain young boy" she recalls standing before.

Narrative Beats

  • Opens with the Wings of Icarus myth as an interpretive frame: Greek mythology "deals abundantly with human frailty" (p.10); Daedalus's wax wings, Icarus's fall.
  • She connects the myth to a boy she stood before "considering what these past few months had brought" — the comparison because "he resembled Icarus" (p.10).
  • Immediately breaks the comparison: the boy "possessed neither the bravery nor the arrogance of Icarus" (p.11).
  • Confesses her provocation as a forced gamble: "I had been pushed into a corner. I had no choice but to do it." — she chose to "incur this boy's divine wrath" and turn his quiet rage toward herself; "The die cannot be returned once cast. The gamble had already begun." (p.11)
  • Volume's second straight non-Ayanokōji cold-open soliloquy (after Sakura in Year 1, Vol. 2 ch1), this time the homeroom instructor's.

Page map note. p.12 carries no prose (illustration/section-break page).

New named entities. none — the chapter names only the mythological figures (Daedalus, Icarus, King Minos, Minotaur) inside the retelling.

Attribution gaps. the boy is never named on pp.10–11; he stays unnamed in the records.

The volume opens inside Chabashira's first person with no setting and no interlocutor — a soliloquy that retells the myth of Daedalus and Icarus and then states why she provoked a certain young boy.

  • Chabashira Sae "For some reason, I thought of Icarus’ wings when standing before a certain young boy."p.10
  • Chabashira Sae "Considering what these past few months had brought, I could say that I made the comparison simply because he resembled Icarus."p.10

  • Narration The myth, her summary: Daedalus, confined in a tower by King Minos alongside his son Icarus, fashions wings of bird feathers, thread and wax; the warning “If you fly too high, the sun will melt the wax holding the wings together. Be careful.”; Icarus forgets it, the wax melts, and he falls into the sea and dies.p.10
  • Trait She frames her own predicament in classical myth — “Greek mythology deals abundantly with human frailty.” — treating her situation as a fable to be interpreted before it is confessed.p.10
  • Inner voice "But I immediately came to realize that the two boys were fundamentally different from one another. This boy possessed neither the bravery nor the arrogance of Icarus."p.11
  • Inner voice "I had been pushed into a corner. I had no choice but to do it."p.11
  • Goal Cornered and out of means, she chose the provocation: incur the boy's divine wrath and turn his quiet rage onto herself.p.11
  • Inner voice "The die cannot be returned once cast. The gamble had already begun."p.11
  • Voice Soliloquy register: measured, literary, fatalistic — she narrates her own decision as a fable already decided, with no hedging and no appeal for sympathy.p.10–11
  • Caveat The boy is never named on these pages — only "a certain young boy" / "this boy". Identity left open; not assigned from context.p.10–11
