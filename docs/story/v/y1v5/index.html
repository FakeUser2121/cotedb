Chapter 1: Sudou Ken’s Soliloquy p.11–12

Setting. No scene — first-person soliloquy, volume cold open. Sudou narrates alone; no other character is present and no school business is named.

Narrative beats

  • Opens on self-assessment: “To be completely honest, I ain’t really a capable person.” — his mother abandoned the family to go into the nightclub business, and he decided to get stronger. (p.11)
  • The father’s hunched back and janitor’s toil sickened him: “Being the idiot I am, I decided to give up on studyin’ and go into sports.” (p.11)
  • Sports path: solo sports first — tennis and pingpong, good but never top-tier — then basketball, which he got into seriously despite not being a team player;
    he kept improving and was recommended to a high school with one of the best basketball teams in the entire country. (p.11)
  • The recommendation was pulled after he got in some trouble, and the setback becomes his self-read: “That was when I really understood the painful truth: I was trash, born from trash.” (p.11)
  • The school choice: “That was why I chose this school.” — a dream school that cost no money and could guarantee even his future. (p.11)
  • Fourth consecutive non-Ayanokōji cold-open soliloquy (Sakura in Year 1, Vol. 2, Chabashira in Year 1, Vol. 3, Karuizawa in Year 1, Vol. 4).

About these pages. Page 12 carries no prose (blank page, page number only). Printed page numbers run ten behind the citations used here (p.11 is printed as page 1), and the translator's watermark sits at every page foot. Sudou's dialect stays as printed (“ain't”, “studyin'”, “nothin'”).

New names. none — the page names no one (the mother, the father, and the recommended high school are unnamed).

Unattributed lines. none — first-person monologue with no dialogue.

Chapter 2: The School Sports Festival Begins p.13–38

Setting. Post-summer review at the crowded café Pallet (back table), then Class 1-D’s afternoon homeroom, the joint Red Team assembly in Gymnasium 1, and Ayanokōji’s walk back to the dorm.

Narrative beats

  • The zodiac debrief (pp.13–15): at Pallet, Horikita, Karuizawa, Hirata and Ayanokōji compare notes on the cruise-ship VIP exam — the rabbit group read alphabetically made Ayanokōji the VIP, the dragon group’s fifth name was Kushida’s — and Horikita offers the Class C theory: a dissatisfied student in Ryuuen’s class must have tossed the wrong answer in defiance. Hirata proposes roping Kushida in as an ally;
    Horikita refuses outright. Karuizawa now calls Hirata Yousuke-kun;
    the no-presence banter ends with Horikita demanding to know if she’s taken for a fool.
  • Chabashira’s briefing (pp.16–25): the school sports festival returns, with heavy physical education from September to early October. Red Team = Classes A and D, White Team = Classes B and C, one team per colour across all grade levels. Individual events score 15/12/10/8 (fifth place −1, one more per place below), recommended events 50/30/15/10 (fifth −2, two more below), a team win 500, the relay three times the recommended values. The handout deducts 100 class points from the first-, second- and third-year batches of the two classes on the losing team;
    grade ranking adds 50 to each grade’s first-place class, nothing for second, subtracts 50 for third and 100 for fourth;
    Chabashira stresses that points move class-by-class, and a team loss plus fourth place costs 200 in total. Individual winners choose 5000/3000/1000 private points or 3/2/1 written-test points (non-transferable);
    last place loses 1000 private points or one test point;
    the overall MVP takes 100,000 private points, each grade’s top three 10,000 each;
    the ten lowest scorers per year lose ten points on the next written exam. Thirteen events, a consensus participation table with a fixed deadline (random assignment if missed), substitutes at 100,000 private points each, health calls left to the student — and the winning team’s pay: “Their reward will be not having any points deducted.” (p.18)
  • The huddle (pp.23–25): Sudou, Ike and Yamauchi pull Horikita into the free homeroom period — Sudou wants every recommended event, Ike wants a personal shot at points, Horikita counters that stamina isn’t unlimited;
    no decisions.
  • The assembly (pp.26–34): Fujimaki takes Red Team command instead of the student council president;
    Katsuragi and Hirata agree to keep out of each other’s way;
    Ryuuen walks Class C out of Ichinose’s cooperation talk;
    Sakayanagi Arisu is introduced — disabled, cane, silver hair, Class A’s other leader with nearly all the followers — and Katsuragi warns Horikita off Ryuuen. Ayanokōji then argues the festival is not a “special exam” (the school never used the term), that preparation beats loophole-hunting, and that Ryuuen likely already has tactics in place — “You’re not thinking big enough,” he tells Horikita.
  • The gate (pp.35–38): alone after class with a file on his phone — whatever it showed, no traps seem necessary — Ayanokōji runs into Chabashira hosing the grounds;
    she warns that without results soon she’ll stop covering for him, and he answers that he’s free to decide his own path.

About these pages. Printed sections 2.1/2.2/2.3 open at pp.16, 26 and 35; the café debrief (pp.13–15) precedes 2.1. Blank or near-empty pages: p.29 (between Sakayanagi's introduction and the Katsuragi–Hirata pact), p.36 (inside the gate dialogue) and p.38; printed page numbers run ten behind the citations used here (p.13 is printed as page 3). Handout blocks at pp.17–22 are exam text, not speech.

Chapter 3: Class D’s Objective p.39–53

Setting. Class 1-D classroom during the weekly two-hour homeroom (Hirata at the blackboard, Chabashira watching from the back), the classroom again as the vote runs, the main entrance after class (Ayanokōji and Karuizawa), and the next day’s physical education period with grip-strength testing.

Narrative beats

  • The two systems (pp.39–40): Hirata chalks “Raising Hands” versus “Ability” and walks through the pros and cons;
    Sudou argues ability and claims every recommended event;
    Horikita cuts in with a supplementary proposal — ability first, but group the strongest and fastest even in the all-participant events.
  • The Shinohara quarrel (pp.40–41): Shinohara objects that slower students lose their shot at private points and test points;
    Horikita tells her the class comes first and that she probably wouldn’t place anyway;
    Sudou turns his new leadership on Shinohara;
    Horikita snipes at Ayanokōji’s phone.
  • Karuizawa, Kushida and the compromise (pp.41–42): Karuizawa objects for the unathletic and calls on Kushida, who asks for a middle-ground strategy;
    Horikita offers one — top placers who don’t need test scores share their private points with the bottom, and the class shares the festival’s gains and losses;
    the girls follow Karuizawa, Hirata calls a majority vote.
  • The vote (pp.43–44): sixteen for Horikita’s plan, thirteen for Karuizawa’s, Ayanokōji abstaining by policy — Class D resolves to win as a class, not as individuals. Sudou claims every recommended competition, to be settled outside if anyone objects;
    Horikita, Kushida and Onodera volunteer;
    about a third of the all-participant spots fill.
  • Kouenji refuses (p.45): Sudou confronts him — “I have no interest in this. You people do what you like.” — and Hirata de-escalates and ends the discussion.
  • The message (pp.46–48, §3.1): Karuizawa waits at the entrance with Ayanokōji’s text — “No matter what, object to Horikita’s plan. Then ask Kushida for her opinion.” — he praises her improvisation, admits he is not personally after Class A, then predicts that one of their classmates will betray the class during the festival. He keeps the Chabashira bargain to himself. Closing thoughts: Katsuragi’s Class A will be solid, and Sakayanagi is dangerous despite the cane — the life-jacket parable.
  • The roster (pp.49–52, §3.2): grip-strength testing — Sudou 82.4 kilograms, Ayanokōji 60.6 (second in the class, behind Sudou’s 82.4 with Kōenji set aside), Hirata 57.9, Ike 42.6;
    tug-of-war orders set by strength;
    rock-paper-scissors picks the scavenger-hunt five, Ayanokōji among them against his wishes;
    Hirata fills every roster and marks it top secret — no photographs.

About these pages. Printed sections 3.1/3.2 open at pp.46 and 49; the blackboard meeting runs pp.39–45 before 3.1. Page 53 is blank (page number only); printed page numbers run ten behind the citations used here (p.39 is printed as page 29).

Chapter 4: Everyone’s Calculations p.54–67

Setting. Class 1-D’s first independent practice on the athletic grounds, the grounds over the following days (aptitude tests, tug-of-war and cavalry coaching, three-legged-race practice), and the mid-September run-up two weeks before the festival.

Narrative beats

  • Watching eyes (pp.54–55): Class A and B students study D from the windows;
    Class C does not look at all, which Ayanokōji reads as Ryuuen already having a strategy he does not need to scout for. Horikita agrees the missing reconnaissance is itself the signal.
  • The name bet (pp.55–56): Sudou calls Horikita “Suzune”;
    she warns him off her first name, and he stakes a bet — first place in the grade gets him the right to use it, failure forbids it forever. She agrees, and raises the bar to the whole grade.
  • §4.1 The leader (pp.57–58): aptitude tests — Sakura finishes last, Sudou is unbeatable, Kushida calls him the sports festival leader and Hirata second the motion;
    Horikita neither encourages nor rejects him, and Sudou accepts.
  • §4.2 The coaching (pp.59–63): Sudou coaches tug-of-war and cavalry, thorough but rash (he kicks dirt at Ike and Yamauchi);
    Hirata fixes the girls’ carriage formation by millimetres. Horikita refuses to teach and boxes Ayanokōji in with questions about whether he is holding back;
    she then fails at three-legged race practice, fighting with Onodera, while Ayanokōji and Hirata pair up smoothly.
  • §4.3 Two problems (pp.64–66): mid-September — Sudou practices without pause, Hirata keeps meeting Katsuragi. Ayanokōji names his two issues: Horikita, who drives every partner away, and Kushida, whose rooftop darkness and hatred of Horikita must be brought into the open. He demonstrates the three-legged lesson himself — set the tempo, let the partner lead.

About these pages. Printed sections 4.1/4.2/4.3 open at pp.57, 59 and 64; the opening ground scenes and the name bet run pp.54–56 before 4.1. Page 67 is blank (page number only); printed page numbers run ten behind the citations used here (p.54 is printed as page 44).

Chapter 5: There’s a Reason for Their Relationship p.68–85

Setting. The school two weeks before the festival (after-class recruitment, Ayanokōji’s dorm room on Saturday morning, the dorm lobby, the athletic grounds during club practice), and the classroom one week out as the participation table is fixed.

Narrative beats

  • The invitation (pp.68–71): Ayanokōji recruits Horikita and Kushida for Saturday “reconnaissance”;
    alone with Horikita he lays out the cruise-ship theory — Kushida may have sold her own VIP identity for private points — and parting in the elevator tells her to decide whether she wants to lead the class.
  • Saturday room (pp.72–73, §5.1): noodles with Sudou, Ike and Yamauchi — the first-name talk, a Sakayanagi rumor question that yields nothing, suspicion about Ayanokōji’s messages, and Sudou’s plan for “Suzune.”
  • The grounds (pp.74–81, §5.2): Kushida and Horikita meet in the lobby;
    at the soccer practice they watch Shibata and then Nagumo outclass both Hirata and Shibata. Kushida drops the pretense — “you were the one who decided to invite me” — and, asked why she and Horikita hate each other, answers “It’s me.” Hirata and Shibata join the group;
    Horikita then asks Kushida the VIP question outright and demands an ally’s answer;
    Ayanokōji leaves the two of them to it.
  • The participation table (pp.82–84, §5.3): one week out, Hirata’s data-driven roster puts Sudou at anchor for the 1200-metre relay;
    Horikita asks for the spot, is pressed for a reason, and reveals the student council president is her brother. Sudou yields, Ayanokōji supplies the “aren’t really on speaking terms” gloss, and Hirata swaps their places.
