System

How the school's machinery is described in the volumes read line by line, entry by entry with page numbers.

Year 1, Volume 1

Vol. 1Initial point grant & core economic rulesp.30–32
  • Rule

    Complete institutional isolation: ANHS is an enclosed boarding campus. Students are prohibited from leaving the grounds or contacting outside individuals (including family and friends) without administrative permission. p.30–31

  • Rule

    Point currency parity: 1 Private Point = 1 Japanese Yen (1 pt = ¥1). All on-campus transactions (cafeteria, convenience stores, mall, equipment, services) are conducted exclusively with points via digital mobile terminals. p.31–32

  • Rule

    Monthly point grant: homeroom teacher Chabashira announces that 100,000 points are deposited into each student's account on the first of every month, ostensibly reflecting their merit and evaluation for passing the entrance exam. p.32

  • Rule

    Transferability & anti-extortion: points may be freely transferred between students via terminals. Coercion, extortion, and theft of points are strictly prohibited and monitored by school authorities. p.32

  • Rule

    Non-convertible & graduation expiration: points cannot be converted into real-world cash, and all unspent points revert to the school upon graduation. p.32

  • Rule

    Dormitory utility policy: dormitories (single rooms, keycard access) do not charge or deduct points for water, gas, or electricity consumption. p.52

  • Detail

    Deliberate administrative trap: the school deliberately conceals the connection between student conduct, class evaluation (Class Points), and monthly deposits, allowing Class D to foolishly assume 100,000 points is guaranteed free money. p.32

Vol. 1Free survival supplies & safety valve mechanismp.106–109
  • Rule

    The zero-point safety valve: the school store features a dedicated lower-shelf section of **entirely free goods**: Free basic sanitary items, toilet paper, notebooks, pencils, and emergency food rations. Allocation rule: students are permitted to take up to one package per month free of charge. Institutional deduction: the school designed this infrastructure from inception anticipating that certain students would exhaust or lose all their private points, ensuring basic survival without cash or points. p.106–109

Vol. 1The S-System unmasked: Class Points, zero-point baseline, & Class A monopolyp.120–130, 138–139
  • Rule

    The S-System evaluation deduction: the monthly deposit is directly tied to the collective evaluation of the class. On May 1st, Class D receives **0 Private Points** due to April disciplinary infractions: **98 absences and late arrivals** **391 incidences of talking or mobile phone usage in class** Total penalty completely wipes out the baseline 100,000 point monthly allowance. p.120–122

  • Rule

    Initial grade-wide Class Point standings (May 1st, 2015): **Class A:** 940 points (94,000 Private Points/student) **Class B:** 650 points (65,000 Private Points/student) **Class C:** 490 points (49,000 Private Points/student) **Class D:** 0 points (0 Private Points/student) p.125

  • Rule

    Proprietary evaluation secrecy: the school refuses to disclose the exact formula or weighted rubric used for point deductions and rewards, comparing it to corporate employee evaluations. p.124–125

  • Rule

    The zero-point trap (the ratchet baseline): stopping negative behavior (tardiness, talking) merely halts further deductions; it does **not** grant positive points. Once a class hits 0 points, it remains at 0 points the next month unless affirmative merit points are actively earned. p.125

  • Rule

    Meritocratic class sorting: students are sorted upon entrance into four tiers based on overall competence. Class A represents the most capable elite; Class D is the bottom depository for failures (chabashira: "You're defective. This is just the result of you being defective."). p.126

  • Rule

    Dynamic class promotion: class letters are not fixed. If Class D accumulates more points than Class C, Class D immediately becomes Class C and vice versa. p.127

  • Rule

    Zero-point survival guarantee: students with 0 points do not starve; housing in dormitories and basic food rations remain guaranteed by the school. p.127

  • Rule

    Red-mark instant expulsion: receiving a single failing score (red mark) on any midterm or final examination results in **immediate, irreversible expulsion** from the school. p.128–129

  • Rule

    Class A monopoly on privilege: the school's renowned guarantee of **100% employment and college matriculation** applies **strictly and exclusively to graduates of Class A**. Classes B, C, and D receive no placement assistance from the school and are left entirely to their own devices. p.129

  • Rule

    Terminal point transfer mechanism: private points can be transferred bilaterally between students directly through their school-issued smartphones by entering the recipient's phone number. p.138–139

Vol. 1Midterm pass threshold & expulsion-avoidance economicsp.152, 173–174, 178, 197–199
  • Rule

    Midterm pass threshold: the exact pass line is **32 points** — scoring at least 32 is safe, anything below is a red mark (→ immediate expulsion). Sudou asks for the exact cutoff; Ike confirms it to him. p.173

  • Rule

    High marks feed class assessment: per Hirata's announcement, strong midterm scores should improve the class's overall evaluation, on top of preventing expulsion. p.152

  • Rule

    Study-group scoring policy: Horikita sets the target at **50 points for everyone**, refusing to coach them to merely skate by at the 32-point threshold ("Aiming to just skate by is dangerous"). p.173–174

  • Rule

    The dead-weight calculus: expelled failures raise the class average test scores without further effort; Horikita argues that dumping the failures leaves only the better students, making a higher class rank simpler to attain — the position she defends to Ayanokōji in their night debate and concedes is "basically correct" only after he proves her blind to others' unique strengths. p.178, 197–199

Vol. 1The old-test gray marketp.229–233
  • Rule

    Past exam papers are openly tradeable: no school rule bars studying from old test problems; point transfers are logged but legal, and barter among students (even across years) is routine enough that a third-year negotiates without surprise. p.230–232

  • Trade

    Ayanokōji buys a third-year Class D student's first-year midterm answer sheet (plus the post-admission mock answers as a bonus): opening offer 10,000 → seller asks 30,000 → AK shows 20,000 → settles at **15,000 points**, transferred in advance (transfer logs serve as mutual anti-fraud insurance). p.230–231

  • Rule

    Zero-point students are the natural sellers: excluded from cafés, they eat the **free vegetable meal set**, making cash points the ideal lever (AK targets his stakeout specifically by watching who buys the free set). p.230

  • Rule

    Old-test value model: identical mock papers across years prove the test bank repeats verbatim — old papers are "insurance," not a guarantee for the midterm. p.232–233

  • Trade

    Distribution strategy: withhold the papers from the trio until the day before the test, then release online to trigger an overnight class-wide cram; Kushida fronts the story (papers "from a third-year student you're close to") so Ayanokōji stays invisible. p.234–235

Vol. 1Buying an exam point (the 100,000-point sale)p.253–254
  • Rule

    Test points are purchasable in principle: on admission students were told they can buy anything with points, and "the midterm test is just one more 'thing' at this school" — Chabashira neither denies the mechanism nor cites any rule against it. p.253

  • Trade

    Price discovery for a single English test point: **100,000 points** ("the exceptional price" for a first-ever request). Ayanokōji and Horikita each contribute via student-ID transfer — combined 100,000 points — and Sudou's failing mark is re-scored to a pass on the spot (expulsion cancelled). p.253–254

  • Fact

    Practical scarcity: no student retains a spare 100,000 points weeks into the term — only a two-payer coalition can afford the transaction. p.253

  • Caveat

    The sale is half-comic but fully binding: Chabashira takes both IDs, collects, and treats the result as official ("There's nothing more to be done."). p.253–254

Vol. 1No points for the worst classp.257–258
  • Fact

    Class D carries the **lowest average score of all four classes by far** after the midterm → Horikita's verdict: no point allocation next month either. p.257–258

  • Fact

    Ground-level poverty: after spending their points, Ike and Yamauchi live off the school's free offerings — the zero-balance survival mode the warning track promised. p.258

  • Rule

    Default expectation set in-voice: don't count on monthly points in Class D; "learn to live a more modest life and give up on points" (Horikita). p.258

  • Caveat

    The class's first explicit long-game framing: point acquisition + exam performance are separate axes — "Studying isn't the sole factor in deciding who goes into what class." p.258

Year 1, Volume 2

Vol. 2July 1: the app mechanics, first payout, delayed depositp.15–20
  • Rule

    School app functions enumerated: Balance Inquiry, personal balance, collective class points, and point transfer to another student; point types suffixed "cl" (class) and "pr" (private). p.15

  • Rule

    "On the first day of every month, they multiplied the cl points, or class points, by 100, then deposited that amount into our private point accounts." p.15

  • Rule

    Zero class points = zero spending money; real currency is refused anywhere on campus; free cafeteria meals are the floor for broke students. p.15–16

  • Position

    Every class admitted with 1000 class points; Class D bled to 0 by May and stayed 0 through June. p.16

  • Position

    July 1 board: Class A 1004 (slightly above admission baseline); Classes B/C each ~+100 over June; Class D 87 — first nonzero total. p.17

  • Rule

    Interpretive precedent (Horikita): a uniform ~+100 jump for all first-year classes reads as the midterm-completion reward, not merit; relative rank, not raw points, drives promotion. p.17

  • Rule

    Deposits can be delayed institution-wide: "The first-year students’ point distribution has been delayed." Chabashira's rider: "Once the trouble has been resolved, you’ll receive your points. If there are still points left, that is." p.18–19

  • Rule

    School supermarket offers free ingredients (same subsidy logic as the free cafeteria set) — Horikita's bento runs on them. p.20

  • Rule

    Disciplinary economics, first instance: an unresolved inter-class fight threatens both an individual summer suspension for Sudou and a class-wide point deduction; the school granted a proof window until "next Tuesday" before ruling fault. p.31–32

  • Position

    Sample drain rate: Ayanokōji's post-midterm-point-purchase balance 8,320 private points; 87 class points would have paid ~8700 yen per student. p.27

Vol. 2Ch.3 §3.1: what a disciplinary case does to a class's balancep.40–47
  • Rule

    The two-headed cost of a fight, stated by the school: "Depending on the degree of responsibility that Class C saddled him with, Sudou could face suspension, and we might see a reduction in our class points." Individual punishment and class punishment are separate dials turned by the same incident. p.40

  • Rule

    Punishment is deferred against evidence, not against pleas: "We’ll make our final judgment next Tuesday, taking into account any eyewitnesses and evidence." A witness is worth a stay of execution. p.41

  • Rule

    The search is institutionally widened, which is itself a cost: "In order to check for witnesses, every teacher will inform their classes of the details of this incident." — the incident's reputation is now school-wide property. p.41

  • Position

    Class D's own arithmetic on the risk, from Ike: "If we lose points because of Sudou, will we be at zero again this month?" — with 87 class points deposited (p.17), any deduction returns the class to zero private income. p.41

  • Rule

    Horikita states the recovery model out loud: "For Class D to rise through the ranks, it’s important to recover the class points we lost as quickly as possible. However, we probably won’t get any points now, thanks to you." — a deduction is priced not as the points lost but as the month's income forfeited. p.44

  • Rule

    The argument she refuses to accept is moral, not economic: "You keep focusing on who started the fight, but that’s a trivial detail." — under this system the school's liability finding, not the truth of who swung first, sets the deduction. p.44

  • Rule

    A witness is only worth what their class position allows: "even if there had been a witness, it’d be checkmate for us if that witness happened to come from Class C. It would be natural for a Class C student to hide the truth in order to protect his or her fellow classmates. This school was a hierarchy, after all." p.47

  • Rule

    Class interest predicts who will help: AK opens with Class B because it is "the class that would most want a witness" — "Between D and C, which class most threatens Class B?" — and skips Class A on the grounds that "I don’t think they’d want to get involved in a troublesome affair that might negatively impact their points." p.61

  • Caveat

    Nowhere in Chapter 3 is a point figure transferred, held in escrow, or offered as inducement; every lever in this chapter is the threat of a future deduction, and no student pays or receives points for the witness search. p.40–61

Vol. 2Ch.4 §4.0: the price of a class change, and the points nobody told Class D aboutp.68–72
  • Rule

    Class change is a purchase, announced by the school itself: "I said that there’s a way to reach Class A without class points." and "I told you on the day you arrived. I said there’s nothing you cannot buy with your points at this school. In other words, you can change classes by using your personal points." p.68

  • Rule

    The price is fixed and stated as a rule, not a rumour: "Twenty million. Do your best to conserve points. Do that, and you can enter whichever class you like." p.69

  • Rule

    Why the number is that high: "If I were reduce the number by one digit, there would probably be over 100 people in Class A come graduation. Then there’d be no point to our system." (sic — "If I were reduce") p.69

  • Position

    The honest ceiling, from the school: "Even if you perfectly maintain your class points upon starting school here, after three years you’d only have 3.6 million. Class A could raise points enough to reach four million. Normally, you simply wouldn’t be able to do it." p.69

  • Position

    Nobody has ever paid it: "Unfortunately, no. No one has done it before." p.69

  • Position

    The record, and how it ended: "About three years ago, one student from Class B saved nearly 12 million points before graduating." — "He was expelled before graduation, however, so he was unable to save those 20 million points in the end. He was engaged in a large-scale fraudulent operation to save up points." p.70

  • Rule

    What the fraud was, and the school's verdict on the motive: "He took advantage of the newly admitted first-year students who didn’t yet understand the system. He went to them one by one and scammed points out of them, so he could reach the 20 million points necessary to move to Class A." — "I don’t think that his goal was particularly bad, but people who break the rules must be punished." p.70

  • Rule

    A second, unadvertised income track exists: "There are cases where individuals may win points based on their efforts in club activities, or their degree of participation. For example, if someone in a calligraphy club were to win a contest, they could receive corresponding points for that award." p.71–72

  • Position

    The information was unevenly distributed by design or by carelessness — "The other classes should have already been well informed of this." — and Class D had not been told at all, three months in. p.71

  • Rule

    The school's position on points-driven club joining: "Do you really think you would’ve been able to see any results after joining a club, results like winning prizes or being in games, with such shallow feelings?" p.71

  • Caveat

    A remembered instance of the club track, told secondhand by Hirata rather than shown: their P.E. instructor Higashiyama-sensei had promised "the student who got the best time during our first class would get 5000 points." p.72

  • Limit

    The scale gap is the real content of the announcement: "For students in Class D, who dreamed of a mere 100 or 200 private points, reaching 20 million was a faraway dream." p.70

  • Caveat

    AK and Horikita are the only two who can verify the claim from experience — "We had tested out that very method ourselves by buying a test point from the school." — the Volume 1 English-point purchase is the private proof behind a school-wide rule. p.68

Vol. 2Ch.4 §4.3–4.4: the cameras that grade you, and the tracks Class D was never told aboutp.95, p.107
  • Rule

    Classrooms are recorded, and the recording is the grading instrument: "The school checks those cameras to see if we talk or fall asleep during class. Otherwise, they wouldn’t be able to accurately assess our monthly performance." — AK locates them physically: "I pointed at the two or so cameras affixed to the ceiling near the corners of the classroom. They were small enough that they wouldn’t be a distraction, and they blended in well with their surroundings, but they were undeniably security cameras." p.95

  • Rule

    The cameras' evidentiary value, established the same day: "Well, there are cameras that record what goes on in the classrooms, right? Therefore, if anything did happen, there’d be evidence of it. Those recordings would blow the Class C guys’ lies away." p.95

  • Position

    The coverage boundary, checked on site: the special building's hallways have no cameras — "There were electrical outlets by the ceiling, but no signs they were being used." — and AK generalises: "It probably wasn’t just the special building. The hallways in the main school buildings probably didn’t have cameras, either." Horikita's own limit case: "If I had to think of places where they wouldn’t be installed, I’d probably say the bathrooms and the locker rooms. Right?" p.98–99

  • Rule

    Club performance is not a private-income side track only; it feeds the class total: "the school evaluates club and philanthropic activities, right? So if he enters a tournament and does well, Sudou-kun could earn points. Those’ll be tied to your class points, too. Wait… Did you guys not know that?" — spoken by a Class B student, and Class D had heard only the private-points half of it. p.107

  • Rule

    The staff side of the same machine, offered as explanation for a teacher's apathy: "Did you know that the school evaluates homeroom teachers when their class graduates?" — Ichinose's claim; the school itself never states it in this volume. p.107

  • Limit

    Nobody in Class D knew about the cameras either: "Seriously?! I never knew that!" and "They’re not easily spotted. I also didn’t notice until they talked about points for the first time." p.95

  • Caveat

    Three system facts in one day (classroom cameras, club points reaching the class total, teacher evaluation) all arrived from a teacher's slip, a student's memory or another class — none of them from an official notice. The information gap, not the point maths, is what Class D keeps paying for. p.95–107

Vol. 2Ch.4 §4.4–4.6: the teacher's own incentive, and the roles Class D never madep.108, p.114–115
  • Rule

    The staff side of the class system, from a Class B student: "the homeroom teacher for Class A gets a special bonus" — reported as Hoshinomiya-sensei's stated motivation, and the first explanation the volume offers for why teaching quality is uneven between classes. p.108

  • Position

    Class D's own reading of the gap: AK on Chabashira — "Our teacher appeared to lack ambition, or even an interest in money. It felt like even if we collapsed into failure, she’d just say it was great." and "Other classes pitied us. If anything, this showed how little interest Chabashira-sensei felt for her own students." p.108

  • Rule

    Classes may invent their own governance with no school mandate: Class B elected a class representative, a vice representative and a secretary "purely informal", "something Class B set up on its own" — and the roles are justified as machinery for the cultural fair and sports festival. p.114–115

  • Position

    Class D has none: "I understood her point, but we certainly weren’t going to assign our own class representative." — the volume's explanation of why Class D organises through four individuals (Hirata, Kushida, Horikita, Ayanokōji) instead of through offices. p.115

  • Caveat

    Neither fact is confirmed by the school; both arrive as secondhand report from Class B. The bonus claim is the volume's only stated material incentive attached to a teacher, and it is never repeated by a teacher. p.108, p.115

Vol. 2Ch.4 §4.6: the first figures any student says out loudp.116–118
  • Spoken

    "I’ve heard rumors that we might be taking a vacation to a tropical island." — the summer reward exists in July only as a rumour a Class B student repeats; the school has announced nothing. p.116

  • Inner voice

    The connection back to Class D's own teacher: "That sparked a memory. I’d forgotten about it, but Chabashira-sensei had mentioned a vacation." p.116

  • Rule

    What Class B expects the break to be worth: "I think there might be a hidden task, one that has a bigger impact on us than the midterms or final exams. Otherwise, the difference between Class A and us wouldn’t close much. We could diminish the gap little by little." p.117

  • Position

    Class B's standing, given freely: "We have about 600 points, so our classes are around 350 points apart." p.117

  • Position

    "So far, the midterm is all that’s given us a chance to raise our class points, so losing at least a few points was unavoidable for us. I mean, even Class A has lost points." — the first statement in the volume that a drop in class points is normal rather than punishment. p.117

  • Position

    Class D's actual month: "Class D had only managed to get 87 points this month. We weren’t anywhere close to being able to compete with the others." p.117

  • Rule

    The narrator's own sizing of the coming event: "It would probably be worth more than 10 or 20 points. However, it was difficult to imagine that it would change things by 500 or even 1,000 points." p.118

  • Caveat

    Every figure in this stretch is a student's report, not a school announcement, and the only two classes whose numbers are compared are B and A; Class C is absent from the account entirely. p.117–118

Vol. 2Ch.4 §4.10: the shops licensed to the school, and what a warranty is worthp.138–140
  • Rule

    "There were some incredibly famous, nationally known stores that did business with our school. Even though their customers were only students and the stores themselves weren’t too very big, they sold items for everyday use and electronic appliances." p.138

  • Rule

    The camera's fault, and its cure: "when the store clerk opened up the camera to confirm the contents, he saw that part of it had been damaged from the fall. That was why the camera wouldn’t turn on properly. Fortunately, because Sakura still had the warranty card, the item could be repaired free of charge." p.139

  • Position

    The wait: the repair is quoted at two weeks, and Chapter 4 ends long before the camera comes back. p.140

  • Rule

    A student's own account of consumer law, used to shield a classmate from handing over her address: "The manufacturer warranty explicitly shows where the item was sold and the date of purchase. Also, I doubt there’d be any legal problems with me putting my information down. It should be perfectly fine if the user’s name is different than the purchaser’s." p.140

  • Feat

    The store accepts it without argument: "N-no, not at all. I understand. There’s nothing to worry about," the clerk said. p.140

Vol. 2Ch.4 §4.10: a price a student actually paidp.143–144
  • Position

    The one hard price named by a student in the volume, in the buyer's own words: "Yes, I most certainly did. It cost 80,000 points. But what of it?" p.143

  • Rule

    That points can be spent on electronics at all is established only by this purchase and by the shopping trip it prompts; no rule about what may be bought, or from which pool, is stated. p.143

  • Position

    The narrator's ceiling, stated to the two girls waiting beside him: "Today was just a preliminary inspection. I don’t have enough points to buy anything." p.144

  • Detail

    The contact is stored in his phone as "Sotomura (Professor)" — students trade points and expertise with each other faster than the school explains either. p.143

  • Caveat

    The thing he is price-checking is not named in Chapter 4; the trip is a preliminary inspection and the purchase is postponed for lack of points. p.143–144

Vol. 2Ch.5 §5.0: paying informants, and paying them anonymouslyp.152–158
  • Fact

    A bulletin-board flyer promises points for information about Sudou and Class C; it is the first time in the volume that students offer their own points as a bounty. p.152

  • Fact

    A school-homepage message board is used to canvass witnesses; the view counter is "still only in the dozens". p.153

  • Fact

    The bounty is Class B's own money, not the school's: "We just decided to do that on our own." p.154

  • Caveat
